# Modelling an electric grand piano

This article discusses physically modelling and simulating a Yamaha CP-80 electric grand piano. I discuss how I got from inspiration to plugin in about 3 days with agentic coding and some physics knowledge. This includes turning qualitative assumptions into important constraints, and navigating an optimisation landscape without truly absolute metrics.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Personal motivation

I was watching a [live show](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rv4wf7bzfFE&t=1858s) of my favourite funk band when i noticed a weird keyboard on stage. It looked like a small grand piano shaped suitcase with the keys attached, and sounded more piano-like than your usual e-pianos.

Due to my severe [G.A.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_addiction#Gear_Acquisition_Syndrome_(G.A.S.)) my second thought was to get one for myself. The cheapest listing in Europe was €5k. I figured the physics shouldn't be that hard and code is cheap as ever, so why not just build one for myself digitally. This article documents the process from a qualitative model all the way to a working, relatively accurate and physically meaningful implementation.
### 1.2 A short history of the electric grand

Experimentation to amplify stringed instruments began in the early 20th century with the rise of the big band era. Guitars acoustically just weren't loud enough. And by the 1940s electric guitars conquered popular music.

 In the 1930s a German physical chemist Walther Nernst already built working grand pianos that projected sound using a loudspeaker instead of vibrating wood inside the instrument itself. (Note that he also won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing the third law of thermodynamics.) Curiously his instruments, the Neo-Bechstein were very similar to the subject of this article: the Yamaha CP-80 which only emerged in the 70s.

In the decades between the two instruments the commercially released electric pianos weren't  stringed instruments, rather the hammers struck metal tuning forks or reeds. These gave a similar touch sensitivity to the piano, but produced a very different sound.

Yamaha designed the CP-80/70 as a replacement of a grand piano for the touring rock musician. The premise was an instrument with the same feel as a top of the line Yamaha piano, superior transportability (you can split it into 2 suitcases),  easy amplification on big and loud stages because of its piezo pickups and a relatively close sound to a proper piano. And because of its electric nature it also carried a lot of the funky and glassy sounds characteristic to 70s funk and 80s disco.

It became really popular at its time, and was used by Paul McCartney, John Lennon, Billy Joel, Pink Floyd and countless others, but mostly on tour or at certain concerts. By todays standards its a very inconvenient instrument, and a MIDI keyboard with a proper sample pack gets you much closer to a real concert piano with superb sound quality. Nevertheless electric grands are important pieces of keyboard history with unique tones that still prevail today.
### 1.3 Physics of an electric grand (qualitatively)

To understand the electric grand piano we have to start with the regular one, and luckily most of our job is omission.

#### 1.3.1 Case and frame

Every part of a grand piano sits in a wooden case on legs. It has two jobs. The curved inner rim is what the soundboard (I will explain later what it is in detail) is glued to, so it sets the boundary for how that panel is allowed to vibrate. The outer case and lid form an enclosure that filters* the sound emitted by the soundboard. A concrete example for filtering behaviour is that a closed lid piano will sound muffled, compared to one with the lid opened. The case itself radiates almost nothing, measurements put it about 20 dB below the soundboard of upright pianos.

Inside the case there is a cast iron frame on which the strings are suspended. It's what carries the ~20 tonnes of tension exerted by the strings. This has no acoustic purpose, and only should serve as a boundary condition for the strings. The main difference between the electric and acoustic versions here is that in an acoustic pianos, there are 1, 2 or 3 strings per note, while in the CP-80 1 or 2. Also the CP-80's bass strings are 67.9 cm against roughly 213 cm on a grand. A significant reduction in length.

*Here, “filter” is used in the signal-processing sense: the case changes the frequency response without creating new frequencies.
#### 1.3.2 The strings

The simplest string model assumes a perfectly flexible string with hinged ends. Its wave speed is independent of frequency, so its standing-wave frequencies are exact integer multiples of the fundamental.

On the contrary one of the most important tonal characteristic of a grand is its *inharmonicity* meaning a nonlinear dispersion relation. (This means that the velocity of the waves is frequency dependent.) This causes the upper harmonics to shift away slightly. For example if you hit a middle C and a C an octave above it, the upper harmonic of the middle C will be a little off from the other ones base frequency creating a natural richness.  This effect can be introduced to the model by adding a term to the differential equation that accounts for stiffness - the resistance of the string against bending.

Interestingly because of the much shorter scale length of the CP-80, the inharmonicity has to compensated by tuning the instrument specially.

With this we get to the last flaw with the textbook string model. The waves on such never decay, which is very obviously non physical. It is easily solved by introducing a velocity dependent dissipative force. Besides this we also use a frequency dependent damping term, which will be discussed later in more detail.

#### 1.3.3 The hammer action

The innovation of the piano was that by pressing keys the string wasn't plucked by a plectrum (harpsichord) but rather the there is a relatively complex mechanical action that launches a hammer to strike the string. This gives superior dynamics control and an improved return mechanism for repeated notes. It is also responsible for the very character and feel of a piano. The CP-80 essentially has the action of a standard Yamaha grand, so the mechanism is pretty much the same.

The string-hammer interaction is the most critical part of our model, since this is the only nonlinear mechanism in play, and it determines the initial harmonic content, so the character of every note. Tuning its parameters right is crucial for both the sound and feel.

It is characterised by the hammers material (felt in case of acoustic pianos, urethane and artificial leather in the CP-80-s case), their weight and attack force. It is best imagined as a weird string-like foam falling onto the string making contact with it for a couple of milliseconds, then getting propelled back.

*Note that a digital model can not reproduce the physical feel of a hammer action. Thus this digital emulation is best used with a MIDI capable digital piano with a hammer action weighed keys.
#### 1.3.4 What actually radiates sound?

In the case of the acoustic grand piano the strings sit on a bridge, a wooden block connected to the soundboard: a large thin wooden sheet that can freely resonate. Think of it as the top of an acoustic guitar or a drum skin. This is the part responsible for radiating sound outwards.

The CP-80 has no soundboard, so its strings produce very little acoustic sound. The sound is amplified electrically and pushed through a loudspeaker. The conversion happens using something called a piezoelectric pickup. Piezoelectric materials have a special property that the polarisation induced in them is coupled to their strain (basically deformation defined in a certain physically meaningful way). This way a piezoelectric pickup sitting under or alongside the strings termination point turns the force exerted on it by the strings vibration to a changing electric field inside it, and so a clear voltage readout.

The lack of a shared soundboard also lets us omit sympathetic resonance between different notes. The exception is a note with two strings: those strings are close enough in pitch and position to interact.

The piezo pickups readout then goes through a filtering circuit (see also 1.3.1) consisting of a bandwidth filter and a three parameter equalizer (using this you can boost or diminish high, low and medium frequencies separately). There is also a tremolo unit, but not the conventional mono tremolo, where the whole signal gets louder and quieter together. Rather the sound moves rhythmically between the stereo channels while the modulation cancels in the centre.
## 2 Physical models

### 2.1 Modelling a string

#### 2.1.1 The differential equation

Let $y(x,t)$ be the transverse displacement of a string of length $L$. Assuming small slopes, $\partial_x y\ll1$, the transverse force balance gives:

$$\mu \,\partial_t^2y=
\underbrace{T\,\partial_x^2y}_{\text{tension}}  
-\underbrace{2\mu b_1\,\partial_t y}_{\text{flat loss}}  
+\underbrace{\eta T\,\partial_x^2\partial_t y}_{\text{internal friction}}  
-\underbrace{EI\,\partial_x^4y}_{\text{stiffness}}  
+f(x,t).  
$$

Here $\mu$ is the linear density, $T$ the string tension, $E$ Young's modulus, and $I=\pi d^4/64$ the second moment of area of a wire with diameter $d$. The term $f(x,t)$ is the external force per unit length supplied by the hammer.

Rather than modelling the losses at the terminations and in the air separately, I represent their approximately frequency-independent part with the term $-2\mu b_1\,\partial_t y$. After projection onto the string's modes, it gives every partial of a given string the same decay rate. The parameter $b_1$ is fitted independently for each string.

The term $\eta T,\partial_x^2\partial_t y$ gives stronger damping to modes with larger wavenumber. It is similar to Kelvin–Voigt damping, where elastic and viscous stresses are added

$$  
\sigma(t)=E\,\epsilon(t)+\eta\,\frac{d\epsilon(t)}{dt}.  
$$
#### 2.1.2 Boundary conditions

We need four boundary conditions for the fourth order equation in $x$. Two are given by the string not being able to move at its terminations: $y(0,t) = y(L,t)=0$. The other two comes from our assumption that the string is hinged rather then clamped. This means that in our model the support at the terminations is point like, this means it can not exert any torque giving $\partial_x^2 y(0,t)=\partial_x^2 y(L,t)=0$. If the terminations were rather clamped, it would mean the strings are held by a block of finite length, this imposes $\partial_x y=0$ while giving arbitrary torque. 

Real terminations sit somewhere between the two, since piano strings bear on agraffes and capo bars that are nearly but not quite point contacts. We take the hinged case, mostly because it is the one that admits a closed-form solution, and it is the common practise in literature.
### 2.2 Modelling hammer-string interaction

#### 2.2.1 The equation for the hammer

We model the hammer as small point-like weight with a compressible padding thrown at the string. The quantity that is determined by the player pressing the keys is the velocity of the key. 

We start with a standard power law contact used everywhere in the piano literature. Additionally we have to make the stiffness (spring-constant in other words) velocity dependent. This is a relatively simple way of modelling the viscoelasticity of the rubber/urethane covering the hammers. Qualitatively: a slowly compressed polymer yields to the stress while a a quickly struck one stiffens up, because the polymers inside the material have no time to rearrange.

Let $x_h(t)$ be the hammer position and $y_s(t) = y(x_0,t)$ the string displacement at the strike point $x_0$. The compression of the facing is:

$$\delta(t) = x_h(t) - y_s(t), \qquad \delta_+=\max(\delta,0)$$

Then with $m_h$ as hammer mass, $K_0$ as absolute stiffness, and $v_{ref}$ as a normalisation term:

$$\;F(t) = K_0\left(\frac{v_0}{v_{\text{ref}}}\right)^{q}\delta_+^{\,p}\;, \qquad m_h\,\ddot x_h = -F(t)$$

There is no direct source for the exponents $p,q$ in this case according to the literature, given the unusual materials used in the CP-80. They are to be fitted later.
#### 2.2.2 The contact patch

A real hammer does not touch the string at one point. Its soft covering pushes against the string over a patch several millimetres wide. We keep the point-like description of the hammer dynamics and put the width into a separate spatial factor, writing the force per unit length as

$$  
f(x,t)=F(t)\,G(x),\qquad \int_0^L G(x)\,dx=1  
$$

Similarly, instead of reading the string displacement at a single point, the hammer sees the patch-weighted average

$$  
\bar y_s(t)=\int_0^L G(x)y(x,t)\,dx\qquad \delta(t)=x_h(t)-\bar y_s(t)  
$$

Note that holding $G$ fixed during the strike is a simplification. In reality, the contact area grows as the facing flattens and shrinks again as it recovers.

The width mainly affects which vibrations the hammer can excite. A string vibrating in a fine, rapidly alternating pattern has neighbouring regions moving in opposite directions. A hammer wide enough to cover both pushes on them at once, so their contributions partly cancel. A wide contact therefore excites low spatial frequencies efficiently and attenuates high spatial frequencies. This is important when fitting the initial harmonic content of the notes.
#### 2.2.3 Contact time

The contact law also gives a characteristic duration for the force pulse. Ignoring the motion of the string, the resulting scaling is

$$
\tau\propto\left(\frac{m_h}{K_0}\right)^{\frac{1}{p+1}}v_0^{\frac{1-p-q}{1+p}},
$$

where the fixed factor involving $v_{\mathrm{ref}}$ has been absorbed into the proportionality constant. Thus higher impact velocities produce shorter contacts, and consequently a different spectral balance. This is the mechanism in the model by which playing harder changes the tone rather than merely the volume.

For C4, the isolated-contact estimate gives roughly $0.7,\mathrm{ms}$. The fully coupled simulation gives about $1.9,\mathrm{ms}$ because the string moves during contact. The estimate is therefore useful as a scale for the interaction, but the actual force pulse is generated by the coupled numerical model.
#### 2.2.4 Other models of rate hardening and viscoelasticity

The impact stiffness can be made velocity-dependent in several ways. The model used here is the simplest of the following three.

| Model                         | Force law                                                                      |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Power law with rate hardening | $F=K_0\left(\dfrac{v_0}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^q\delta_+^p$                  |
| Hunt–Crossley                 | $F=K\delta_+^p(1+\alpha\dot{\delta})$                                          |
| Stulov-type hereditary model  | $F=K_e\delta_+^p+z(t)$, $\dot{z}+\dfrac{z}{\tau_f}=K_f\dfrac{d}{dt}\delta_+^p$ |

The Hunt–Crossley model multiplies the elastic force by a factor involving the instantaneous compression rate. Since this factor changes sign between loading and unloading, the model produces a dissipative difference between the two parts of the contact. Its coefficient of restitution can therefore depend on the impact velocity.

The Stulov model adds a memory variable with an exponential relaxation. The force depends not only on the current compression but also on the recent compression history. Yielding to a more realistic hysteresis loop and an asymmetric force pulse.

In our model, the impact velocity is sampled once and used to set the stiffness. There is no contact damping or hysteresis, so the contact is lossless and time-symmetric. I tried the more detailed models while tuning the parameters, but their additional complexity did not improve the result enough to justify using them.
### 2.3 Piezoelectric pickups

Piezoelectricity couples the mechanical and electrical responses of a material. In the linear regime, mechanical stress produces electric displacement, while an electric field produces strain. For the pickup's readout, we only need the direct piezoelectric effect; the converse coupling from electric field to mechanical strain will not be modelled. Since the notes use the same type of pickup, we can discuss the electrical relationship for a single element.

The relevant constitutive equation is

$$  
D_i=\sum_{j,k}d_{ijk}\sigma_{jk}  
+\sum_j\varepsilon_{ij}^{T}E_j,  
$$

where $\sigma$ is the mechanical stress, $E$ the electric field, and $D$ the electric displacement. The permittivity $\varepsilon^{T}$ describes the ordinary dielectric response, while $d$ contains the piezoelectric coupling.

Almost all of this tensor structure is irrelevant for the approximation used here. Let the $1$-axis be the direction normal to the electrodes and along the relevant loading and poling direction. Assuming mainly longitudinal loading, the equation reduces to a scalar equation:

$$D=d\sigma  +\varepsilon E. $$

Let $A$ be the electrode area and $h$ the thickness of the pickup. If the stress is approximately uniform,

$$  
F_b=A\sigma,  
\qquad  
Q=AD,  
$$

where $F_b$ is the force transmitted to the pickup and $Q$ is the charge on one electrode. Taking $V$ as the voltage across the element gives $E\approx -V/h$, and therefore

$$  
Q=d\, F_b-C_pV,  
\qquad  
C_p=\frac{\varepsilon A}{h}.  
$$

The CP-80 has a high-impedance buffer, which means that very little current leaves the electrodes, so charge is approximately constant: $\dot Q=0$, consequently,

$$  
C_p\dot V=d\,\dot F_b,  
$$

and, ignoring a constant voltage offset:

$$  
V_{\mathrm{oc}}(t)=\frac{d}{C_p}F_b(t).  
$$

The input circuit is not perfectly open. Leakage through the pickup and the buffer can be represented by an effective resistance $R_{\mathrm{eff}}$ in parallel with $C_p$. The resulting electrical model is

$$  
C_p\dot V+\frac{V}{R_{\mathrm{eff}}}  
=d\,\dot F_b.  
$$

In the frequency domain,
$$
\frac{V(s)}{F_b(s)}=\frac{d}{C_p}  
\frac{s}{s+1/(R_{\mathrm{eff}}C_p)}.
$$

The pickup therefore behaves like a force-to-voltage converter followed by a high-pass filter. Its cutoff frequency is

$$  
f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi R_{\mathrm{eff}}C_p}.  
$$

The force itself comes from the transverse component of the string tension at the termination:

$$F_b=\pm T  \left.\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L}$$

If two strings belong to the same note, their contributions are added. Any fixed mechanical factor caused by the bridge geometry can be absorbed into the overall gain. The equalizer and stereo tremolo are applied after this pickup model.
## 3 Solving the model
### 3.1 Modal decomposition
#### 3.1.1 Obtaining the modal equation

The string has hinged boundary conditions, and so it is natural (every function in the series satisfies $y=0$ and $\partial_x^2y=0$ at both endpoints) to expand it as a Fourier sine series on the interval $[0,L]$: 

$$  
y(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_n(t)\sin(k_nx),\qquad k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}.  
$$

With the coefficients:

$$  
q_n(t)=\frac{2}{L}\int_0^Ly(x,t)\sin(k_nx),dx.  
$$

$2/L$ comes from normalisation:

$$  
\int_0^L\sin(k_nx)\sin(k_mx),dx=\frac{L}{2}\delta_{nm}.  
$$

The spatial derivatives act on each sine function like so:

$$  
\partial_x^2\sin(k_nx)=-k_n^2\sin(k_nx),\qquad \partial_x^4\sin(k_nx)=k_n^4\sin(k_nx).  
$$

Accordingly substituting the Fourier series into the string equation gives:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mu \,\partial_t^2y=
T\,\partial_x^2y
-2\mu b_1\,\partial_t y
+\eta T\,\partial_x^2\partial_t y
-EI\,\partial_x^4y
+f(x,t) \\[6pt]
\Downarrow \\[6pt]
\mu\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left[\ddot q_i+\left(2b_1+\frac{\eta T}{\mu}k_i^2\right)\dot q_i+\frac{Tk_i^2+EI k_i^4}{\mu}q_i\right]\sin(k_ix)=f(x,t)
\end{gathered}
$$

Multiplying by $\sin(k_nx)$ and integrating from $0$ to $L$ isolates the $n$th term through orthogonality:

$$  
\frac{\mu L}{2}\left[\ddot q_n+\left(2b_1+\frac{\eta T}{\mu}k_n^2\right)\dot q_n+\frac{Tk_n^2+EI k_n^4}{\mu}q_n\right]=\int_0^Lf(x,t)\sin(k_nx),dx.  
$$

Let $M=\mu L$ be the total mass of the string, and define

$$  
\omega_n^2=\frac{Tk_n^2+EI k_n^4}{\mu},\qquad \sigma_n=b_1+\frac{\eta T}{2\mu}k_n^2.  
$$

For the hammer force distribution $f(x,t)=F(t)G(x)$, we define coupling coefficients as:

$$  
c_n=\int_0^LG(x)\sin(k_nx),dx.  
$$

The modal equation finally becomes:

$$  
\ddot q_n+2\sigma_n\dot q_n+\omega_n^2q_n=\frac{2c_n}{M}F(t).  
$$
#### 3.1.2 Change of parameters


The modal frequencies are:

$$  
\omega_n^2=\frac{Tk_n^2+EI k_n^4}{\mu}=n^2\omega_0^2(1+Bn^2),\qquad \omega_0=\frac{\pi}{L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}},\qquad B=\frac{\pi^2EI}{TL^2}.  
$$

Here $\omega_0$ is the fundamental angular frequency that the string would have if it had no bending stiffness. This equation clearly shows the *inharmonicity* of the piano discussed earlier.

We can express $B$ in terms of the string's dimensions with  $f_0=\omega_0/(2\pi)$ (the real fundamental frequency is $f_{\mathrm{fund}}=f_0\sqrt{1+B}$ ) and $I=\pi d^4/64$ as:

$$  
B=\frac{\pi^3Ed^4}{256\mu L^4f_0^2}.  
$$
If we take $f_{fund}\approx f_0$ , we can see that $B\propto L^{-4}$. The CP-80 bass strings are approximately $67.9,\mathrm{cm}$ long, compared with roughly $213,\mathrm{cm}$ on a concert grand. The ratio of lengths is about $3.14$, so the short scale alone would increase $B$ by approximately $3.14^4\approx97$.

Wound bass strings reduce this difference because the winding increases the linear density while the bending stiffness remains largely controlled by the core. In the fitted strings, the increase is closer to a factor of $20$.

The damping rates are

$$  
\sigma_n=b_1+\frac{\eta T}{2\mu}k_n^2=b_1+b_3\,\omega_n^2,\qquad b_3=\frac{\eta}{2(1+Bn^2)}.  
$$
#### 3.1.3 Free string solution

Once the hammer leaves the string, $F(t)=0$:

$$  
\ddot q_n+2\sigma_n\dot q_n+\omega_n^2q_n=0.  
$$

Using $\omega_{d,n}=\sqrt{\omega_n^2-\sigma_n^2}$ , if the hammer leaves at time $t_0$, the subsequent motion of mode $n$ is

$$  
q_n(t)=e^{-\sigma_n(t-t_0)}\left[q_n(t_0)\cos\left(\omega_{d,n}(t-t_0)\right)+\frac{\dot q_n(t_0)+\sigma_nq_n(t_0)}{\omega_{d,n}}\sin\left(\omega_{d,n}(t-t_0)\right)\right].  
$$

Another useful thing to consider is the corresponding discrete-time recursion, with the discrete time written as $t_k=k\tau$ and $q_n^k=q_n(t_k)$:

$$  
q_n^{k+1}=2e^{-\sigma_n\tau}\cos(\omega_{d,n}\tau)q_n^k-e^{-2\sigma_n\tau}q_n^{k-1}.  
$$

This is an exact recurrence for the free mode at the sampling times. During hammer contact, a force-dependent input term is added to the same recurrence.
### 3.2 Hammer coupling

For a rectangular contact patch of width $a$ centred at $x_0$,

$$  
c_n=\frac{1}{a}\int_{x_0-a/2}^{x_0+a/2}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right),dx=\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x_0}{L}\right)\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{n\pi a}{2L}\right),  
$$

where

$$  
\operatorname{sinc}(z)=\frac{\sin z}{z}.  
$$

The first factor depends on the strike position. For example, a point strike at $x_0=L/7$ does not excite the seventh mode in this idealised model. The second factor describes the cancellation caused by the finite contact width. Its first zero occurs at $n=2L/a$, when the patch spans one wavelength of the mode.

The hammer feels the patch-weighted displacement

$$  
\bar y_s(t)=\int_0^LG(x)y(x,t),dx=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_iq_i(t).  
$$

Recalling the hammer model,

$$  
\delta(t)=x_h(t)-\bar y_s(t),\qquad F(t)=K_0\left(\frac{v_0}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^q\delta_+(t)^p,\qquad \delta_+(t)=\max(\delta(t),0).  
$$
$$  
m_h\ddot x_h(t)=-F(t).  
$$

we can assemble the final system of equations describing hammer-string interaction with adding the string:

$$
\left\{
\begin{gathered}
F(t)=K_0\left(\frac{v_0}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^q\max\left(x_h(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_iq_i(t),0\right)^p
\\[6pt]
m_h\ddot x_h(t)=-F(t)
\\[6pt]
\ddot q_n+2\sigma_n\dot q_n+\omega_n^2q_n=\frac{2c_n}{M}F(t)
\end{gathered}
\right.
$$

#### 3.3 The pickup signal

The piezo pickup reads the transverse force at the string termination:

$$  
F_b(t)=\pm T\left.\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L}=\pm\frac{\pi T}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^n n q_n(t).  
$$

If two strings belong to the same note, their bridge forces are added before applying the pickup model. The electrical high-pass, equalizer, and stereo tremolo from section 2.3 are then applied to $V(t) \propto F_b(t)$. This gives the concrete output of the model.
### 3.4 Numerical simulation
#### 3.4.1 Modal truncation

The Fourier expansion is infinite, but the implementation retains the first $N$ modes:

$$  
y_N(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}q_n(t)\sin(k_nx).  
$$

The displacement seen by the hammer is approximated by

$$  
\bar y_{s,N}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_nq_n(t).  
$$

The spatial truncation is the first numerical approximation. The number of modes must be large enough that increasing $N$ no longer changes the bridge signal or the audible output too much. 

#### 3.4.2 One contact step

At this point, the continuous system has been reduced to a finite set of coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations. The remaining step is to discretise time.

Let $t_j=j\tau$, where $\tau$ is the internal timestep. For readability, we use a single index $i=1,\ldots,M$ for the resonator. The index $i$ is an implementation index, not a physical string index; each slot has its own $c_i$, $\omega_i$, $\sigma_i$, $\beta_i$, and bridge weight $w_i$.

$$
x_h^j=x_h(t_j),\qquad v_h^j=\dot{x}_h(t_j),\qquad q_i^j=q_i(t_j).
$$

The state also contains a contact flag $\chi^j$, a damper flag $\chi_d^j$, and a contact-age counter $a^j$ used by the numerical timeout.

At each contact step, the string displacement at the hammer position is evaluated from the current modal state:

$$y_s^j=\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_iq_i^j,\qquad \delta^j=x_h^j-y_s^j.$$

The rate-hardening factor uses the positive initial hammer speed for the current strike, not the updated velocity $v_h^j$:

$$K_{\mathrm{eff}}=K_0\left(\frac{v_{\mathrm{init}}}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{q}.$$

The contact force is

$$
F_{\mathrm{raw}}^j=\begin{cases}K_{\mathrm{eff}}\max(\delta^j,0)_+^p,& \chi^j=1\\ 0,& \chi^j=0\end{cases}
$$

The hammer is advanced with semi-implicit Euler. The velocity is updated first, and the new velocity is then used for the position update:

$$v_h^{j+1}=v_h^j-\frac{\tau}{m_h}F^j,\qquad x_h^{j+1}=x_h^j+\tau v_h^{j+1}.$$

For each emitted resonator, let $\sigma_i$ be its current damping rate and define

$$\omega_{d,i}=\sqrt{\omega_i^2-\sigma_i^2},\qquad \rho_i=e^{-\sigma_i\tau},\qquad \theta_i=\omega_{d,i}\tau,$$

$$\beta_i=\frac{2}{m_{\mathrm{total}}}c_i\tau\rho_i\frac{\sin(\theta_i)}{\omega_{d,i}}.$$

The modal recurrence is taken from the free string case and it is updated with a force term (see appendix):

$$q_i^{j+1}=2\rho_i\cos(\theta_i)q_i^j-\rho_i^2q_i^{j-1}+\beta_iF^j.$$

The force is evaluated before the modal update, so only the current sample $F^j$ appears in the recurrence. Once contact has ended, $F^j=0$ and the modes continue with the free-string recurrence.

The contact age is incremented after each contact sample:

$$a^{j+1}=a^j+1.$$

The implementation clears contact when the current compression has disappeared and the updated hammer velocity is directed away from the string, or when the timeout is exceeded:

$$\chi^{j+1}=0\quad\text{if}\quad\bigl(\delta^j\leq0\ \text{and}\ v_h^{j+1}<0\bigr)\ \text{or}\ \bigl(a^{j+1}>a_{\max}\bigr),$$

with $a_{\max}=2000$ internal samples.

After the modal update, the bridge-readout signal is

$$F_b^{j+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_iq_i^{j+1}.$$

The precomputed bridge weight $w_i$ contains the termination sign $(-1)^n$, the partial factor $n$, the unison or pickup-polarisation weight, and the normalized bridge scale. The coupling coefficient $c_i$ contains the strike-position and finite-width factors.

The implementation does not store the physical factor $\pi T/L$ literally. Its normalized bridge factor is equivalent to

$$S_{\mathrm{bridge}}=\frac{(m_{\mathrm{total}}/n_{\mathrm{strings}})f_0^2}{m_{\mathrm{ref}}f_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}.$$

The code folds this factor into the bridge readout weights and applies the remaining per-voice calibration through `outGain`. Thus $F_b$ denotes a normalized bridge-readout signal rather than a quantity retained in newtons.

When the key is released and the sustain pedal is not holding the note, the damper flag changes to $\chi_d=1$. The intended effective damping for each emitted resonator is

$$\sigma_{i,\mathrm{eff}}=\sigma_i^{(0)}+\chi_d\,\sigma_{\mathrm{damper}}.$$

The modal states are not reset; the existing oscillation continues with the modified poles. In the current implementation, damper release rebuilds the pole coefficients but retains the note-on input coefficient `bIn`; therefore $\beta_i$ is not recomputed at release. The current release path also does not retain the fast partner's note-on damping multiplier.

#### 3.4.3 Double strung notes

A double-strung note is not represented by two independent modal banks. The note specification doubles the total vibrating string-group mass, so the total hammer force is applied to one lumped bank. In the symmetric case this is equivalent to applying $F^j/2$ to each physical string: with single-string mass $m_s$, the separate-string input scale is $(2/m_s)(F^j/2)$, while the lumped-bank scale is $(2/(2m_s))F^j$.

The bridge contribution is then doubled through the output weights. The normalized bridge factor is computed from the single-string mass, $m_{\mathrm{total}}/n_{\mathrm{strings}}$, while the resonator weights contain the factor $n_{\mathrm{strings}}$. Thus the second string contributes through the total mass, the summed bridge output, and the unison frequency split; it does not have a separate state or an explicit string--string coupling path.

For a partial with $n\leq k_{\mathrm{TwinBelow}}$, the bank emits a second resonator slot. Both slots receive the same force sample, but they have separate modal frequencies, damping coefficients, and bridge weights. For double-strung notes, the extra slot carries the note-specific unison frequency split and helps reproduce the beating and two-stage decay of the unison. These slots are a reduced representation of the physical string and transverse-mode structure, not a literal one-resonator-per-string construction. For single-strung notes, the current value of $k_{\mathrm{PolarHz}}$ is zero, so the two slots are frequency-degenerate and differ only in damping and bridge weight.

For $n>k_{\mathrm{TwinBelow}}$, only one slot is emitted. Its bridge weight is the sum of the two partner weights, preserving the nominal bridge weight while discarding the fine beating above the cutoff. The low-partial slots remain separate states, but both are driven by the same $F^j$ and do not exchange energy.

When the key is released and the sustain pedal is not holding the note, the damper flag changes to $\chi_d=1$. The intended effective damping for each emitted resonator is

$$\sigma_{i,\mathrm{eff}}=\sigma_i^{(0)}+\chi_d\,\sigma_{\mathrm{damper}}.$$

The modal states are not reset; the existing oscillation continues with the modified poles. In the current implementation, damper release rebuilds the pole coefficients but retains the note-on input coefficient `bIn`; therefore $\beta_i$ is not recomputed at release. The current release path also does not retain the fast partner's note-on damping multiplier.

### 8.2 The analytical contact time derivation

If we assume that the string does not move, we can calculate the contact time analytically. This gives a useful reference for understanding the model's parameters and where they matter. Omitting the string gives:

$$  
m_h\,\ddot{\delta}=-K\delta^p  
$$

The stiffness $K$ is constant during one impact. Multiplying by $\dot{\delta}$ turns both sides into total derivatives:

$$  
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}m_h\dot{\delta}^{2}\right)=-\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{K\delta^{p+1}}{p+1}\right)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{1}{2}m_h\dot{\delta}^{2}+\frac{K\delta^{p+1}}{p+1}=\frac{1}{2}m_hv_0^2  
$$

At maximum compression, $\dot{\delta}=0$, so all the kinetic energy is stored in the compressed facing:

$$  
\delta_{\max}=\left(\frac{(p+1)m_hv_0^2}{2K}\right)^{\frac{1}{p+1}}  
$$

Solving the energy equation for $\dot{\delta}$ and eliminating $K$ through $\delta_{\max}$ leaves a trajectory whose shape depends only on $p$:

$$  
\dot{\delta}=v_0\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\delta}{\delta_{\max}}\right)^{p+1}}  
$$

The contact lasts twice the time to maximum compression, since the motion is time-reversible. Writing $dt=d\delta/\dot{\delta}$ and substituting $s=\delta/\delta_{\max}$ gives:

$$  
\tau=\frac{2}{v_0}\int_0^{\delta_{\max}}\frac{d\delta}{\sqrt{1-(\delta/\delta_{\max})^{p+1}}}=\frac{2\delta_{\max}}{v_0}\int_0^1\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{p+1}}}  
$$

The remaining integral is a Beta function. Substituting $u=s^{p+1}$ gives:

$$  
\int_0^1\frac{ds}{\sqrt{1-s^{p+1}}}=\frac{1}{p+1}\int_0^1u^{\frac{1}{p+1}-1}(1-u)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,du=\frac{1}{p+1}B\left(\frac{1}{p+1},\frac{1}{2}\right)  
$$

Therefore,

$$  
\tau=\frac{2\delta_{\max}}{v_0(p+1)}B\left(\frac{1}{p+1},\frac{1}{2}\right)  
$$

For $p=2$, the dimensionless integral factor is

$$  
\frac{1}{3}B\left(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2}\right)\approx1.402  
$$

In the model,

$$  
K=K_0\left(\frac{v_0}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^q  
$$

Substituting this into $\delta_{\max}$ and collecting the powers of $v_0$ gives the scaling that matters:

$$  
\boxed{\tau\propto\left(\frac{m_h}{K_0}\right)^{\frac{1}{p+1}}v_0^{\frac{1-p-q}{1+p}}}  
$$
### 8.3 Recurrence derivation

$$  
\ddot q_n+2\sigma_n\dot q_n+\omega_n^2q_n=0.  
$$

With the ansatz: $q(t)=e^{rt}$ the characteristic equations roots are:

$$  
r_{\pm,n}=-\sigma_n\pm i\omega_{d,n}.  
$$

Let $t_k=k\tau$ and write $q_n^k=q_n(t_k)$. The general solution sampled at these times is

$$  
q_n^k=C_{+,n}e^{r_{+,n}\tau k}+C_{-,n}e^{r_{-,n}\tau k}=C_{+,n}z_{+,n}^k+C_{-,n}z_{-,n}^k,  
$$

 Our recurrent relationship will be:
 $$  
q_n^{k+1}=(z_{+,n}+z_{-,n})q_n^k-z_{+,n}z_{-,n}q_n^{k-1}.  
$$

 To verify the recurrence, substitute three consecutive samples:

$$  
\begin{aligned} 
&q_n^{k+1}-(z_{+,n}+z_{-,n})q_n^k+z_{+,n}z_{-,n}q_n^{k-1}= \\ &C_{+,n}z_{+,n}^{k-1}\left[z_{+,n}^2-(z_{+,n}+z_{-,n})z_{+,n}+z_{+,n}z_{-,n}\right] \\
&+C_{-,n}z_{-,n}^{k-1}\left[z_{-,n}^2-(z_{+,n}+z_{-,n})z_{-,n}+z_{+,n}z_{-,n}\right]=0.  
\end{aligned}  
$$

The final equality holds because each bracket factors as

$$  
z_{\pm,n}^2-(z_{+,n}+z_{-,n})z_{\pm,n}+z_{+,n}z_{-,n}=(z_{\pm,n}-z_{+,n})(z_{\pm,n}-z_{-,n})=0.  
$$

Using

$$  
z_{+,n}+z_{-,n}=2e^{-\sigma_n\tau}\cos(\omega_{d,n}\tau),\qquad z_{+,n}z_{-,n}=e^{-2\sigma_n\tau},  
$$

gives

$$  
q_n^{k+1}=2e^{-\sigma_n\tau}\cos(\omega_{d,n}\tau)q_n^k-e^{-2\sigma_n\tau}q_n^{k-1}.  
$$
